My favourite TV show is the Great British Baking Show (or the Great British Bake Off, as it’s called in the UK). It’s a truly lovely show where contestants are doing their best to bake delicious goodies, occasionally making disastrous mistakes like using salt instead of sugar, dropping a cake on the floor, or not proving their bread dough for long enough. When disaster does strike, there is usually a supportive air of camaraderie and sympathy. And of course, another reason that I love watching it is that I have a very sweet tooth, I enjoy baking, and am partial to a slice of cake!

But as well as making their food taste good, the contestants always have to make sure that it looks appealing. You know when you walk past a baker’s and you see the tempting little cakes in the window - you want to eat them!

So I love this line in our reading from Jeremiah this morning: “Your words were found and I ate

them”. It makes me imagine this delightful experience of an inviting morsel that you just can’t help eating. Think of wanting to ingest and consume God’s word as if we could literally devour it like our favourite dinner.

There are other occasions when the Bible depicts the word of God as being physically edible. The prophet Ezekiel is given a scroll to eat, which he says tastes sweet as honey. The same thing happens to John in the book Revelation, and the Psalms also refer to the word of God as being sweeter than honey.

But the takeaway from all these verses aren’t that we should go home and eat our bibles for lunch. Instead it illustrates the idea that we should be so drawn to God, so attracted to the way of life and love that Jesus demonstrated, that we want to absorb it with our whole beings. It brings us back to the idea that we are what we eat, and this applies to more than just literal food. It also includes every type of thing we consume. What we read, what we listen to, what we watch, who we surround ourselves with, everything we admire and emulate. God wants us to consume things of God, and in this way become more like God ourselves.

In our gospel reading Peter is learning this slowly. He has spent months hearing Jesus’ words, but he still has trouble absorbing what they mean. Last week he was called the rock, and now today he has this fall from grace. But even now, Christ keeps calling him, and perhaps that is another part of consuming God’s word. It isn’t enough for the words to sound sweet or look lovely in the window. It requires a choice to repeatedly return to it - a choice to stick to a diet of holiness. God’s words have to enter us deeply enough to change what comes out of us.

You remember a couple of weeks ago when Jesus described how it is what comes out of the heart that defiles, rather than failing to observe handwashing purity rules. The flip side is also true, that it is what comes out the heart that is of God. This is what Paul is talking about as he describes the practical outworkings of being a person of faith. He describes how we are changed by God, how we demonstrate what our diet is, by what we put out into the world. “Let love be genuine”, says Paul. And that is the key to everything.

Genuine love shapes everything else. It is how we know what to do when we confront evil or apathy. It is how we might know what to do with parts of the Bible that seem confusing or contradictory. Genuine love is much more than appearance or easiness. It is love that has soaked all the way through. Love that is baked into our souls so that it comes out of our mouths and shows us to be people of God’s word.

Paul is building on this with his list of instructions. “Bless those who persecute you.” Do not repay evil for evil. If your enemy is hungry, feed them; if they are thirsty, give them something to drink. Then he quotes the strange image of heaping burning coals on an enemy’s head. But rather than him meaning that revenge is ok as long as it’s done kindly or through inflicting guilt, the direction is shaped by the instruction to genuine love. Even burning coals can be good, as they are necessary to keep a fire going, so some scholars even think this is a reference to bestowing life-giving resources on others. But whatever the exact meaning, it must be shaped by genuine love.

We are to do what gives life. We overcome evil with good. We are to consume, to eat, God’s word and God’s love, until it pours out of us, bearing good fruit. No matter who or what else we are surrounded by, we can refuse to let another person's actions or attitudes decide what kind of person we will be, and instead be shaped by the genuine love of God.

And just as we have to eat multiple times a day to stay nourished, we have to bake a continuous diet of God-ness into our lives. We don’t pray once and think, ‘alright, that’s done with’, instead we pray every day. We don’t read a bible passage once and tick it off, instead we return to it, chew on it, inwardly digest it. We don’t gather for church once a year, we return to each other as the community of Christ’s body. And in this way, through these choices made again and again, we instill in our hearts what Peter still has to wrap his head around - that following Jesus is a lifelong practice that encompasses anything and everything we might go through.

So this morning as we return again to the table where we celebrate the Eucharist, the imagery becomes wonderfully literal. We will eat the bread and wine, and we are drawn into Christ. We learn and feel and absorb for ourselves that God doesn’t simply tell us to love, but also feeds us with love. In this way we become more and more fully the Body of Christ: hands that serve, voices that bless, hearts ready to rejoice with those who rejoice and weep with those who weep.

We won’t do it perfectly. Peter went through highs and lows, stumbling again and again, and we do the same. The Church does the same. But still, Christ calls us, and God’s grace is continuously held out to us.

So remember, you are what you eat. Yearn for the word of God, find it, take it, eat it. Let your hearts overflow with what they are fed with, and may that be the genuine love of God, sweeter than honey, soaked into the depths of your soul.

Amen.

Jeremiah 15:15-21; Romans 12:9-21; Matthew 16:21-28
