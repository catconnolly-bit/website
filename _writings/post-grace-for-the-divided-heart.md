I always enjoy it when you can really feel a personality coming through in the bible passages for the day, and we get a strong dose of personality from both Paul and Jesus in our readings this morning.

When you imagine Jesus - the real person, not just the idea or a picture in a kids bible - I wonder what he’s like in your head? It could be easy to imagine him as gentle, serene, perhaps with a soft voice and agreeable manner. And of course, Jesus is gentle. He tells us so himself: ‘I am gentle and humble in heart’. But gentle doesn’t mean bland or humourless, and in this passage, I think Jesus sounds like he’s leaning into a bit of sarcasm.

John the Baptist is in prison, and his disciples have just approached Jesus to ask if he is the Messiah. They leave with their answer (a little cryptic but essentially a ‘yes’), and now Jesus is talking to the crowds of people who have been following him around and have witnessed this interaction. And he basically tells them that they’re behaving like inconsolable toddlers who don’t like any of the options they’re given.

‘We played the flute, and you didn’t dance; we wailed, and you didn’t mourn’. In other words, nothing makes you happy. Just look, Jesus is saying, at how you are responding. John the Baptist lived in the wilderness, fasted, and called people to repentance - people said ‘He has a demon!’. Then Jesus comes eating and drinking, and people said, ‘Look, a glutton and a drunkard!’. You can almost hear Jesus raising his eyebrows at them.

‘Perhaps,’ Jesus says, ‘could it be, that the real issue is not me or John?’ Perhaps, heaven forbid, the problem is that people have already decided that they don’t want to hear what God is saying.

Unfortunately, our humanness teaches us that this is a common attitude. We can always find a reason not to respond to God. The messenger is too serious, or not serious enough; the invitation is too demanding or too simple; the call repentance is too harsh, the offer of grace is too generous. We find ways to critique the tone, the timing, the method, the personality, and never quite have to truly confront the deeper question: is God speaking to me?

Then we have Paul, in our reading from Romans, taking us even deeper into our persistent human dividedness. ‘I don’t understand myself,’ he says. ‘I don’t do what I want, but I do the very thing I hate!’ I think this whole passage is one of the most honest descriptions of the spiritual life in the whole Bible.

I wonder how familiar that sounds to you. I think most, if not all, of us know something about that gap between what we intend and what we actually do. As the parent of a very nearly three-year-old, I find myself regularly facing my own inconsistency. I mean to be patient 24/7, but sometimes she just drives me crazy! I mean to get her to eat all her vegetables with every dinner, but sometimes she ends up having ice cream without finishing her carrots. We mean to be generous in spirit, and then we find ourselves clinging to control, to certainty, to our own way of seeing things. We mean to trust God, and then anxiety takes over.

And the thing to remember is that Paul’s conundrum is not a sign of someone who doesn’t care or isn’t trying hard. He is describing himself as someone who loves the good, and sincerely wants to do what is right. But he is driven to frustration because the human heart is fickle, and even our most devoted determination is not enough by itself.

Again, I wonder how familiar that sounds to you. Many of us try very hard. We try to be good, to be strong, to be faithful, try to hold things together and carry our load. But even the greatest religious effort is not enough to rescue us from ourselves, as Paul laments.

But here we see God’s mercy in Jesus’ tender invitation of hope. After the sarcastic little nudge, Jesus draws us in, saying ‘Come to me, all you who are weary, and carrying heavy burdens, and I will give you rest’.

He doesn’t say, “Come to me once you have finally sorted yourself out.” Or, “Come to me when you’ve become the kind of person you always meant to be.” Instead Jesus says, “Come to me, all you that are weary.” Come exactly as you are right now. Come with your burdens, your failures, your frustrations. Come with that gap between what you long to be and what you are.

I wonder how many of you have seen a pair of oxen yoked together in real life? I don’t think I ever have, and perhaps it’s due to our unfamiliarity with the reality of this image that our first reaction is to think a yoke sounds unpleasant, like another burden we don’t want to add to what we already carry. But Jesus isn’t asking us to accept another great weight on our shoulders. In fact it’s the opposite. The purpose of a yoke is to join two together. It is a means of sharing a task, carrying responsibility equally alongside the other. To be yoked is to be pointed in the same direction, undertaking the same job, doing it together.

So far from saying, ‘come to me and I’ll give you a great load to bear’, Jesus is instead offering to join with us on the journey, to be inescapably linked to us, side by side. The yoke he offers is the fruits of the Spirit, the grace of God, the love of Christ, keeping us connected to the one who is gentle and humble in heart, who can walk with us along any road, no matter how full of potholes made of our own struggles and shortcomings.

In a world which is constantly pressuring us to take on other yokes - those of perfection, reputation, control, fear, or self-sufficiency, this is very comforting. A yoke which orients us to Christ, to mercy, truth, love, and forgiveness of self and others is a great gift. It lightens our own burdens because it frees us from the efforts of trying to make it by ourselves. Not, of course, that things become effortless, but that we find ourselves always within the circle of grace that invites us to remember the path we are on, and who we walk next to. Thus we are saved by the faithfulness of Christ.

There’s a lot in today’s passages which feels relatable and rings true. You probably know what it is to feel weary from trying, from failing, from carrying a heaviness no-one else can see. You might recognise yourself in the crowds who always find a reason to resist God’s message. We share in Paul’s frustration of wanting the good but not always being able to do it. For all these reasons and more, today’s gospel is for you. Christ is here, offering rest for your soul.

The word ‘gospel’ means good news, and the good news for today is not that our struggles are imaginary. They are real. And Paul is right, that the human heart is divided. We don’t always do the good; we repeat what we meant to leave behind; we aren’t always as patient, trusting, generous or faithful as we hope to be.

But what is stronger than all of it is grace. The good news is that Jesus meets us on the road, coming alongside us in the dust of ordinary life, and says, “Take my yoke upon you, and learn from me.” Grace not earned by the spiritually successful, but poured out upon the weary, the burdened, upon you and me. So thanks be to God through Jesus Christ our Lord: the one who knows us, goes with us, and gives us rest.

Amen.

Romans 7:15-25a;  Matthew 11:16-19, 25-30
