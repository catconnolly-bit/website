Today’s readings as assigned by the lectionary are pretty heavy going. None of them leave you with an uplifting joyful feeling, and Jesus says some things in this gospel passage from Matthew that quite frankly, we would rather that he hadn’t said.

This isn’t exactly the bit of the gospel we would wave from the rooftops and say ‘hey look, read this and believe in Jesus!’. Instead I think if your non-church friend or family had finally come along with you one Sunday, and this was the reading, you’d be sinking in your seat.

But thank goodness, our services include sermons, for the exact reason that we can think through some of the difficult stuff, and listen for how God’s voice is speaking even through our discomfort.

So some context is always helpful. In the previous few chapters of Matthew’s gospel, Jesus has been travelling around the countryside visiting various towns and villages, teaching and preaching to whoever is listening. But once we get to chapter 10, the audience changes. Here, we are in the middle of a long conversation just between Jesus and his disciples. And for these twelve chosen, called, and clueless dudes, it’s the serious pep talk they need. The work of evangelism, fearless proclamation of God’s kingdom, is now in their hands. And for them to be able to go out and do what Jesus is asking of them requires a bit of tough love preparation.

But perhaps the most important theme which ties it all together, and sweeps through our other readings today too, is that of remaining focused on God above all else. This focus is why Jesus sends the disciples out without the comforts of extra clothes or money, as we heard last Sunday. They are to go with only the clothes on their backs, because their security rests in God, not in what they can carry.

This focus on God is why Jesus warns them to expect tension, strained or broken relationships, even among family members, because even their nearest and dearest must not take precedence over their relationship with and reliance on God. And while we may pay lip service or give theoretical assent to the idea of God above all else, when it comes down to the nitty gritty of real life we often find such commitment grating.

One example of this is that St Francis’ father was not too pleased at his son developing devout inclinations. Francis came from a wealthy merchant family, and his father expected him to uphold the family business and status. But as Francis became more serious about following Christ into poverty, and did things like give away goods and money from the business, his father saw it not as beautiful holiness but as irresponsibility, waste, and public embarrassment. And yet, Saint Francis is now one of the best known Saints, because of his commitment and focus on God.

So Jesus warns the disciples. He tells them the truth that prioritising God above everyone and everything else comes with a real cost, perhaps even the cost of close family relationships, at the cost of respect, at the cost of comfort, at the cost of even their lives.

We see some of these costs mirrored in the reading from Jeremiah, and the Psalm, today too. We see that an unusually committed faith is never easy, and yet, despite that, praise is woven through the story as well. The prophet Jeremiah writes of singing to the Lord, and the psalmist declares that the Lord’s love is kind. Their stories are full of challenges, and yet a light shines through.

So let’s go back to the gospel reading. Perhaps the one message Jesus is hoping will stick in the disciples’ hearts is his favourite phrase. For despite the dire warnings, he tells them, ‘do not be afraid’.

Fear is a powerful influence on us human beings. I wonder what you fear. Perhaps the very things that Jesus warns might happen. And those are valid fears. But the thing to keep in mind is that fear tells us to make ourselves smaller; to keep everyone else happy even at the cost of our own integrity or well-being. Fear is persuasive because it often disguises itself as wisdom: do not risk too much, do not say too much, do not let your faith become inconvenient. But Jesus says ‘do not be afraid’. Not that bad things won’t happen, not that they are immune to disaster, but instead to keep their focus instead on God, on the light, on the faith that sustains and remains through anything else.

This is also the context for the difficult line where Jesus says he hasn’t come to bring peace, but brings a sword instead. At face value this goes against the Jesus we think we know, the Jesus who definitely advocates in favour of peace. But viewed through the lens of fear, it perhaps makes more sense. Here Jesus is not encouraging violence, but is talking metaphorically about the sharp edges of decisions, the painful points of where focus on God causes a rift in the focus on others.

The peace Jesus is rejecting here is not the peace of God, but the surface level peace we so often tend towards, that keeps everything smooth without awkward conversations, disagreement or challenge. But what we often mistake here for peace is often only silence, fear wearing the cloak of good manners. The real peace of Christ is deeper than that.  It is the wholeness that begins when truth is spoken with love, when what is hidden is brought into the light, and when God is trusted more than appearances. Such peace can be costly, and yet Jesus invites our focus to remain on God, and to not be afraid.

When I was young I collected beanie babies, and one of my favourites was a red cardinal. On moving here I was delighted to see the real thing, and 4 years later it still feels exciting to catch a glimpse of the red feathers as a cardinal goes flitting by. But do you know what isn’t exciting? A sparrow. They are not eye-catching, or unusual, or large. So Jesus’ illustration of a sparrow being known by God remains relatable even 2000 years later. If this bird that goes almost entirely unnoticed by most people can be of value to God the Creator and sustainer of life, then how valuable are you, each one of us, worth many many sparrows and known down to the hairs on our heads?

That is the tenderness at the heart of this difficult gospel. Putting God above all else does not mean God as a tyrant, demanding loyalty and disregarding pain. Instead the God who calls us to costly faithfulness is the God who knows us down to every detail. The God who asks everything of us is also the God who sees everything and holds us in love. So perhaps the courage Jesus nudges us towards is not the absence of fear. Perhaps courage is remembering whose we are when we are afraid.

As Paul says in the reading from Romans, we live through Christ, and therefore walk in newness of life. Sin does not own us. Death does not own us. Fear does not own us. We belong to Christ.

So the disciples are sent out, with no guarantee of safety, but the promise of God's presence. And that promise is enough to begin. Hopefully, it is enough for us too. When faithfulness costs something, when love requires truth, when fear urges us to retreat into silence, Christ calls us back to the center, which is God above all else. Not because nothing can hurt us, but because nothing can separate us from the love of God.

You are worth more than many sparrows, so do not fear, even when the challenges of this life are great. May we walk in newness of life, trusting that the God who calls us is faithful, the Christ who leads us has gone before us, and the Spirit will give us courage for the next faithful step.

So there’s an exploration of some hard passages, and a light of hope to hold onto. Perhaps a spark of joy flickered through after all.

Do not be afraid.

Amen.

Romans 6:1b-11;  Matthew 10:24-39
