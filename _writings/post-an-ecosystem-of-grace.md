I wonder how many of you have a word that you really don’t like. It might be the sound of it, or the image it brings to mind, or something about how it feels to say it. A word that’s just a bit ‘ew’. For a lot of people, it’s the word ‘moist’. My apologies if that made you shudder! For me, the word I really don’t like is the word ‘flesh’. I don’t like it at all. And this morning, as you may have noticed, Paul uses it a lot. Ten times, in fact, in just a few verses.

Now when Paul is talking about the flesh—living according to the flesh, or setting the mind on the flesh—I think we usually automatically assume he’s talking about our literal physical bodies. It can sound as though he’s saying that our bodies are bad, and unfortunately Paul has often been read that way. However, the Greek word he is using here is the word *sarx*. *Sarx* CAN mean physical flesh, but more often when Paul uses it, including here, it is instead describing an entire way of life. It’s Paul’s way of talking about an ecosystem, a way of living which is organised without the presence of God. A life shaped by fear instead of trust, or self-protection instead of love.

By contrast, when it comes to talking about our physical selves, Paul uses another word, *soma*, which simply means ‘body’. And for Paul, the *soma*, the body, is spoken about positively. For example, later in Romans Paul uses *soma* when he says to present your bodies as a living sacrifice, making the body the very site of worship. Then, in the first letter to the Corinthians, he calls our bodies temples of the Holy Spirit. So it’s important to know that our bodies are not the enemy in Paul’s theology. They are part of God’s good creation. Jesus heals bodies, feeds bodies, touches bodies, suffers in a body, and is raised bodily from the dead. So the Spirit does not rescue us from being embodied. Rather, the Spirit teaches us how to be fully alive.

But *sarx*, or "flesh" as he talks about in today’s reading, is a different matter. It is a whole way of inhabiting life. Paul is talking about what we set our minds on, what we give our attention to, and what atmosphere we breathe. And Paul is not simply urging us to make better choices. He is announcing that, in Christ, God has already opened up an entirely new way of living, making the question not simply, "Can I behave better?" but rather, "Which ecosystem am I inhabiting?"

We all have various ecosystems that we live in. Some of them are obvious. We have families, churches, workplaces, and neighbourhoods. But there are quieter ones too, that we may not even notice because we have lived inside them for so long.

If you think about what an ecosystem is, it is a network of relationships where everything affects everything else. When it is healthy, life creates the conditions for more life. But unhealthy ecosystems do the opposite. And these worlds we exist within have the power, little by little, to reinforce patterns so that they become normal without us even noticing.

It is all too easy for us to function within unhealthy frameworks. For example, our phones can be a negative ecosystem. We don't usually pick them up intending to become anxious. We simply want to check the news, catch up with friends, or fill a quiet moment. But without thinking about it, one headline leads to another. One comparison or angry voice leads to the next. And before we know it, we begin expecting conflict. We assume the worst about people. Not because we consciously chose anxiety, but because we have been breathing it all day.

Or, consumption can become an ecosystem. The story that says we are what we own, or what we achieve, or how secure we can make ourselves. We find ourselves discontent, always searching for enough, which always seems just out of reach.

This is what Paul means when he talks about ‘the flesh’. Not simply a bad decision here or there, but a whole atmosphere that behaves as though God is absent and we must save ourselves.

But it’s important to notice the good news at the beginning of today’s passage: "There is therefore now no condemnation for those who are in Christ Jesus." Paul reminds us that God has already acted. We are gifted grace, not guilt, because the alternative Paul describes is an ecosystem of life, formed through the Spirit.

In this reality, grace makes honesty possible because we no longer have to defend ourselves. Honesty makes confession possible. Confession opens the door to forgiveness. Forgiveness restores trust. Trust leads to gratitude which overflows into generosity. Life creates the conditions for more life. So to live into the Spirit is to live into who God created us to be - as summarised by the Westminster catechism, ‘to glorify God and enjoy him forever’.

Sometimes we won’t get it right. In Jesus’ parable, I think we know what it is to embody each of those different soils within ourselves.

There are days when we are the path: tired, distracted and guarded, and the good news held out to us is brushed aside. There are days when we are rocky ground, receiving hope with joy but finding that when trouble comes we turn too easily from God's presence. There are seasons when thorns take over: worry, ambition, grief, and the thousand small demands of life.

And then, by the grace of God, there are moments when we are good soil. Something clicks. Our soul is attuned to the Spirit, and we find ourselves able to trust, to forgive, to begin again. The hope of this parable is not that we might manage to make ourselves constantly perfect, but instead that the sower is so generous in their sowing. God doesn’t wait until every stone is cleared or all the weeds are removed. Instead grace is scattered with holy extravagance, in the knowledge that life can emerge even where nobody expects it.

So how do we tend to our ecosystem? The questions to reflect on might be: What is shaping me? What receives my attention? What stories do I absorb and retell?

The life that the Spirit draws us into comes with gifts to nourish us along the way. Prayer opens us to being receptive to the nudges of God; the good news gives us a story of grace to inhabit. And church is a community where we can grow together, nurturing life among each other. Together, we become another kind of body, the gathered flourishing of worship where the Spirit can be at work.

And all of this begins in love. No matter what our spiritual environment is at this point on the journey, no matter what soil we might be this morning, change is always possible, because Christ has already come near, and the Spirit dwells within us.

I suspect I will always have an internal shudder at the word ‘flesh’. But Paul uses it to help us recognise the atmospheres around us, to notice where we are trying to live independently of the presence of God. And then we are invited to pause, to notice that in Christ, God has already opened another way to live. May we bear much fruit as the Spirit nurtures us in this new way of being, breathing grace into our souls, and giving us life in abundance to the glory of God.

Amen.

Romans 8:1-11;  Matthew 13:1-9,18-23
