Growing up, our home had Bible verses scattered all over it. There were daily verse calendars, cross stitches, colouring sheets, artistic doodles, and laminated print-outs. And while it’s not so common in the UK, here in the US those verses also spill out into the general culture that surrounds us. There are some Bible verses that simply demand to be put on a refrigerator magnet or bumper sticker: “The Lord is my shepherd.” “God is love.” “I can do all things through Christ.” And then, there is Isaiah this morning: “Those who live on the earth will die like gnats.”

If Hallmark ever produces an Isaiah 51 range, I suspect sales may be somewhat limited. But, hope is not lost, because Isaiah is doing something much more hopeful than it first sounds.

This part of Isaiah comes from the world of the Babylonian exile. Jerusalem had been conquered. The Temple had been destroyed. People had been killed, scattered, and deported. The institutions that had told the people of Judah who they were - the land, Temple, monarchy, nation - had collapsed. And for people whose faith was profoundly connected with Jerusalem and Zion, this was not simply a political crisis. Their religious world had come apart.

Then into that world the prophet Isaiah says: look back. Look at our history. Look at the example of Abraham and Sarah. God began with one old, unlikely couple and created a whole people, whose very existence is evidence that God has been faithful before. Then Isaiah says: look up. Look around. Look at the heavens. Look at the earth. Even these things, the things that appear utterly permanent, as concrete as possible, are not permanent. The heavens vanish like smoke. The earth wears out like an old shirt. And yes, apparently, we all die like gnats!

But, as easy as it is to be sidetracked by this stark pronouncement, the takeaway is actually this: everything we think is permanent may fail, but God’s salvation does not.

That is what Isaiah wants the message to be. He isn’t saying ‘everything will be fine’, because it hadn’t been - Jerusalem had fallen, people had died, exile was real. But the stronger message is that even when everything is definitely not fine, God is still God. Even when the structures of our world that we thought were permanent crumble around our ears, God is still God. Even when futures we imagine disappear in a puff of smoke, God is still God. And even at the end of the world, and the end of us, God is still God. Salvation and deliverance are not contingent on things unfolding how we think they should. Salvation endures.

But what is astonishing is that we are not just spectators or passive recipients of this salvation, of God remaining God through all things. Instead we are key players in the story, and we see this invitation to join in through our gospel reading.

Today we hear about Jesus telling Peter - hot-headed, wholehearted, faithful Peter - that the whole church will be built on him as the foundation. It’s a glorious moment when Peter raises his hand in class and has the correct answer. Jesus asks ‘Who do you say that I am?” Simon Peter answered, “You are the Messiah, the Son of the living God.” And Jesus answered him, “Blessed are you, Simon son of Jonah!”’ And then Jesus says to Peter that he will build his church, and even the gates of Hades will not prevail against it.

This is one of our recurring themes - death doesn’t get the last word. We’re all going to die, perhaps even in an undignified way like a swatted insect, but we are in a story which is larger than our fragility. What matters is that we are caught up in something larger than ourselves - the community of Christ.

What I also love is what happens next with Peter. This week he is the rock, but all of three verses later, which we will hear next Sunday, he is told, ‘Get behind me Satan! You are a stumbling block to me’. Talk about highs and lows!

But this is really enormously reassuring, because God's plans, God's work in the world, and the very church itself, are not reliant on human beings getting everything right. And isn't that a relief?

I wonder if you've ever felt anxious about doing something because you knew you might not get it perfectly right. We are all too aware of the fact that we make mistakes. We sometimes misunderstand things or misspeak. The church itself will sometimes be brave and faithful and sometimes fearful and foolish. We will occasionally proclaim Christ beautifully, and then five minutes later say or do something spectacularly unhelpful.

But God will still be God. And the wondrous truth is that God uses us as we are, flaws and all, to do incredible things. We are the very vessels of God's Spirit, and the Spirit burns with light and love even through our cracks and flaws.

In our reading from Romans Paul urges us to present ourselves, our physical bodies, as a living sacrifice. What he means by this is to orient ourselves to Christ, to become, together, the Body through which the world encounters something of the living Christ.

Because we may be only human, but we are God's own creations, the bearers of good news and grace-filled love. You are only human, but your hands, your feet, your voice, your time, your gifts - these are ways in which God becomes tangible in the world.

We are messy mortal creatures, as changeable as Peter, but we are each imbued with gifts of grace - to feed someone, to teach, to encourage, to visit, to give, to speak when something is wrong, to forgive, to make room, to show compassion. God’s faithfulness to us, and God’s willingness to entrust this work to us, calls forth an embodied response. And nobody is left out. We are all in the image of God, stamped with a spark of the divine, and we all have gifts to live into that play a role in God's kingdom.

How often are you aware of being an integral part of God's work in the world?

Perhaps not often enough. We tend to imagine the work of God as something done by particularly holy people, unusually gifted people, people other than ourselves. But Paul’s picture of the Body of Christ needs all of us, precisely because we each have our unique selves to bring. God takes ordinary, imperfect human lives and makes something enduring from them.

So there is good news running through all the readings this morning. Isaiah says: the world and humanity are fragile, but God’s salvation endures. Matthew says: even death cannot defeat what God is doing, and human failure cannot derail it. Romans says: therefore, offer yourselves. Become part of it.

Not because everything depends upon us. Not because we will get everything right. But because the God who called Peter, Abraham and Sarah, the God who spoke hope to exiles, the God revealed in Jesus Christ, is still at work.

The heavens may vanish like smoke. The earth may wear out like an old shirt. We may all die like gnats. But God always is and will be God. And until then we get to be God’s hands and feet in the world. We get to tell the greatest story ever told, woven through with our own experience of hope and grace-given love.

I want to close with a prayer traditionally attributed to St. Teresa of Avila:

“Christ has no body but yours,

No hands, no feet on earth but yours,

Yours are the eyes with which he looks

Compassion on this world,

Yours are the feet with which he walks to do good,

Yours are the hands, with which he blesses all the world.

Yours are the hands, yours are the feet,

Yours are the eyes, you are his body.

Christ has no body now but yours,

No hands, no feet on earth but yours,

Yours are the eyes with which he looks

compassion on this world.

Christ has no body now on earth but yours.”

Amen.

Isaiah 51:1-6; Romans 12:1-8; Matthew 16:13-20
