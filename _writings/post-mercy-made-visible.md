In our gospel reading, Jesus tells the Pharisees to go and learn what is meant by the phrase, ‘I desire mercy, not sacrifice’. To us this might sound simple, or obvious, but in the context of that day and culture, it is asking for a shift in mindset.

If I asked you what the word ‘sacrifice’ meant, what would you say? Maybe “Giving something up.” “Putting someone else first.” “Doing something hard because it matters.” or “Choosing love over convenience.” Sacrifice is a word that we don’t use much these days, but at its heart it means a cost. It means being willing to lay aside time, comfort, money, security, even life itself. But in the Bible, sacrifice is not just about being willing to let go. At its best, sacrifice is love made visible. It is a way of saying, ‘This matters more.’

And it’s a theme which has endured into modern day culture.

In The Lion, The Witch, and the Wardrobe, the lion Aslan makes the ultimate sacrifice. He offers himself in the place of Edmund, a boy who has made some poor choices and prioritised turkish delight over his family. Aslan’s sacrifice is an exchange of lives, exemplifying the verse from John chapter 15 which says, ‘No one has greater love than this, to lay down one’s life for one’s friends.’

Or a quite different example of sacrifice is in the book Little Women, where the girls all give up their Christmas Day breakfast to take it to a poor family who don’t have any food. If you start searching for it, you’ll find sacrifice everywhere.

What it looks like has changed though. In the Old Testament, sacrifice was very concrete. They sacrificed animals, and not just any animal, but the best, the firstborn, the most valuable one. In their lives, these animals were food, clothing, and security, so to give them to God was to offer something deeply costly. It is a deep expression of trust, faith, and relationship with God.

But as so often happens, somewhere along the way, people kept losing sight of the more important underlying meaning, and only held onto the outward symbol, turning the act of sacrifice into a hollow husk of what it was supposed to be.

So we arrive at the prophet Hosea, telling the people that what God really desires is steadfast love, not sacrifice. Hosea is explaining that sacrifice without mercy, without love, is pointless. What God wants from us is much keeper than a gesture, instead God desires covenant love. Faithful love. Mercy that shapes the heart.

Now on to Jesus. I wonder if you’ve ever noticed that all the people Jesus calls to follow him are actually quite busy doing something else. When Jesus sees Matthew, a tax collector busy at his job, he says ‘Follow me’, and Matthew gets up and walks away from his life as he knows it. Matthew may be a tax collector, distained by his people because of association with Rome, betrayal, dishonesty and stealing, and yet he leaves his entire life behind. His whole self becomes an offering, a sacrifice of love because Jesus came near and called him by name.

This is the God we still worship ourselves, this God who desires mercy and steadfast love, not sacrifices devoid of meaning. This is the difference between our faith being just an idea or an action.

If we know how to appear outwardly Christian but not how to draw near in love and mercy, something has gone wrong. It’s all too easy to be very busy being religious while missing the God who is sitting at dinner with sinners.

As usual, we can look to Jesus for a concrete example of the love and mercy God wants to see from us too. Dinner is set aside when the plea comes from a leader of the synagogue for his dead daughter to be raised, and while on the way Jesus encounters the woman who has been bleeding for twelve years. These two are of very different social status, one being a respected public figure, the other being an unclean nobody ostracised from normal interaction. Yet Jesus treats them both the same. He greets them both with a yes, with a willingness to give his time, attention, and relationship.

This is mercy. Not vague kindness. Not simply feeling sorry for someone from a comfortable distance. Mercy is love that moves toward need. Mercy is love that lets itself be interrupted. Mercy is love that refuses to let shame, status, illness, reputation, or even death have the last word about a person. Because every person is a neighbour. Every person is beloved. Every person is in the image of God.

So here we see what it is to sacrifice in the way that God desires. Here we see what Jesus means when he asks for mercy, what Hosea means when he talks about steadfast love. It is the offering of ourselves, in response to the mercy already given to us.

What is your sacrifice? What are you willing to give? God seeks our hearts, seeks our relationship, seeks our response. A Christian life shaped by faith is a life that is willing to stop on the way to something important, because another beloved child of God has reached out. Being willing to say yes when Jesus calls, even if we’re busy doing something else. Being someone who loves their neighbour no matter who they are. It is a gift of ourselves. A wholehearted orientation towards love. And because it isn’t easy, because it goes against the grain of our inclination to put ourselves or only our nearest and dearest first, this merciful love is a sacrifice. Giving ourselves to God is our costly, living, ongoing sacrifice that we choose to make, again and again.

In the Eucharist we call this a sacrifice of praise and thanksgiving. We come as people who need the physician: with gratitude and wounds, faith and uncertainty, lives which are sometimes steadfast and sometimes a bit wobbly. Still Jesus calls. Still Jesus eats with sinners. Still Jesus stops for the one who reaches out trembling. Still Jesus takes the dead by the hand. And God asks us for our sacrifice of love.

So perhaps the question for us today is not simply, “What will I give up?” but “Where is mercy asking something of me?” Where is love asking for my time, my attention, my patience, my courage? Where is Jesus calling me away from the safe and familiar, as he called Matthew from the tax booth? Where is someone reaching out, trembling, hoping that they might be seen and met with love?

Because the sacrifice God desires is not empty religion, not performance, not correctness without compassion. It is the offering of a heart made tender, vulnerable, and open by grace. It is mercy received becoming mercy given. It is love that interrupts us, changes us, and sends us out again.

So come to the table this morning as you are: steadfast or shaky, grateful, uncertain, beloved. Receive all that Christ is, offers, and calls us to be. And then go and be that mercy, that steadfast love, in the world.

Amen.

Hosea 5:15-6:6;  Romans 4:13-25;  Matthew 9:9-13, 18-26
